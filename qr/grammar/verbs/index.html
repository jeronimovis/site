This workbook started in my own classes. For a few months I listened carefully to my students, not to what they said (I'm always interested in their work, their trips and their opinions) but to how they used verbs. And I noticed something that worried me.

Most of the mistakes I heard were not with difficult grammar. They were with structures my students had studied many times and knew perfectly well. In a written test on the third-person -s, the past simple or Did you see…?, they would get almost everything right. But when they spoke, the same structures broke.

So I decided to look at the data. I went through the transcripts of many classes with B1, B2 and C1 students: more than 58,000 words of student speech in total. I marked every verb mistake I could identify clearly. I found 326. The students were Spanish speakers from Argentina, but I think the patterns are common to many Spanish-speaking learners of English, and probably to you too. Here's what the data showed:

  • The biggest problem is telling past events in the present. "Yesterday I have a meeting…", "We sell it in two hours." About a quarter of all the mistakes are of this type. It's very common at B1 and B2, and almost disappears at C1.
  • The second is the third-person -s. "The company have…", "Everyone say…". It's the most widespread mistake: two out of every three students made it, at every level.
  • Next come agreement and verb patterns: "people is", "avoid to use", "a tool for change".
  • At C1 the mistakes are different. Tense is mostly under control, but some old habits remain: "Did you saw…?", "I don't know what is the difference", "If I had the money, I will…".

The most interesting finding isn't what goes wrong but when. The mistakes rarely appear in short answers. They appear in long turns, when someone is telling a story, explaining how something works in their job, or giving a strong opinion. In those moments attention goes to the content, and the grammar runs on autopilot. Very often, the correct form appears in the same sentence as the wrong one. The rule is known. The problem is control under pressure.

That's why this workbook isn't a grammar course. It reminds you of what you already know, shows you the situations where it breaks, and then makes you use it in exactly those situations.

How to use this workbook
  • Do one lesson at a time. Don't rush through three in an evening.
  • Do every exercise before you press Show answers. Check your answers afterwards and notice which ones you got wrong.
  • The last two exercises of each lesson are writing tasks. Take your time with them. They are the most important part.
  • If you have the chance to speak English during the week (in a class, with friends, at work, or even talking to yourself), try to listen to yourself and pay attention to that lesson's structure. If you can record yourself telling a short story, even better. Noticing your own mistakes is the first step to fixing them.

The lessons go from simple to complex. If you are C1, you can go quickly through the early ones, but don't skip them completely. Some of the most persistent mistakes are in Unit 1.

Lesson 1 be and there is / there are / there will be

Levels○ B2○ C1

Focusam / is / are; was / were; there is / there are; there will be; there was / there were

The key idea in one lineFor existence, the subject is always there, never it: there will be, not it will be.

The Spanish pullHabrá gente↓There will be people

1Think about

  • When you describe your city or your office, how do you say hay? Do you change it for plural nouns?
  • How do you say va a haber or habrá in English? Do you ever say it will be?
  • What's the past of there is? And of there are?

2You already know

  • There is + singular or uncountable noun; there are + plural noun.
  • There changes tense like any verb: there was / there were / there will be / there has been / there would be.
  • was / were agree with the subject: you were, they were, he was.
Spanish vs English
SpanishEnglish
Hay gente que… (never changes)There are people who…
Va a haber / Habrá dos bandas (no subject)There will be two bands
Había muchos problemasThere were a lot of problems

The key idea. Habrá has no subject, but English needs one. For existence, the subject is always there, never it.

  • It will be fun describes something we've already mentioned.
  • There will be food introduces something new.

3Why it goes wrong

  • You're describing a future event, like a trip, a conference or a party. Habrá comes out as it will be, because it will be is a chunk you've said thousands of times. There will be is much less familiar.
  • You start the sentence before you've chosen the noun. There is… comes out automatically, like hay, and then the noun turns out to be plural: there is… people.
  • You're generalising about trends. There is a lot of people who… sounds natural because a lot of feels singular.
  • You're reporting what someone said, and was comes out with you or they.
Typical mistakes
  • "I'm sure it will be a lot of people at the event." → there will be a lot of people
  • "There is many options for that." → There are many options
  • "There was three meetings yesterday." → There were three meetings
  • "You was right." → You were right

Lesson 2 Third-person -s

Levels○ B1● B2 most frequent○ C1

Focushe / she / it + -s; has and does; organisations, systems and indefinite pronouns as subjects

The key idea in one lineAsk yourself: could I replace the subject with it or he / she? If so, add the -s.

The Spanish pullTodo el mundo dice↓Everyone says

1Think about

  • When you explain how your company or a tool works, what is the subject of your sentences? Is it usually he or she?
  • Do you say everyone say or everyone says? Which one sounds right to you, and why?
  • Which verbs are hardest for you in the third person: regular verbs, or have and do?

2You already know

  • Present simple: he / she / it + verb-s.
  • Irregular forms: have → has, do → does, go → goes.
Spanish vs English
  • In Spanish every person has its own ending (uso, usás, usa, usamos…), so the third person isn't special. You never have to remember it.
  • In English, -s is the only person ending left. It's small, it comes at the end of the word and it adds no meaning, so it's the first thing to disappear when you're concentrating on content.
  • Todo el mundo dice and cada persona tiene are singular in Spanish too. The problem is that everyone sounds plural in English.

The key idea. Change the question you ask yourself. Don't ask "is it he / she / it?". Ask "could I replace the subject with it or he / she?"

SubjectReplace withVerb
The bankituses
Everyonehe / she (one person, many times)says
A person who…he / shesees
The companyithas
That ideaitmakes

In relative clauses, look back at the noun before who / that: a person who has, people who have.

3Why it goes wrong

  • The subject isn't a person. You drilled the -s with he / she / it, so subjects like the bank, the app, the government or the AI don't set off the "third person" alarm.
  • The subject feels plural. Everyone, everybody, each person and nobody describe many people, so the verb comes out without -s.
  • The subject is far from the verb. In a colleague who works with me, your attention is on who, and the verb loses its connection with colleague.
  • have and do are special. has and does aren't made by adding -s. They are separate words you have to retrieve, so they're the verbs most often left bare.
  • You can't hear it. In fast speech, you don't notice that you've dropped it. The same person often says talks and talk a few seconds apart.
Typical mistakes
  • "The app send you a notification." → sends
  • "Someone always tell me the same thing." → tells
  • "I have a colleague who work from home." → works
  • "The company have an office in Chile." → has

How Mercado Libre works

Almost everyone in Argentina has a Mercado Libre story. Someone buys a second-hand bicycle, someone else sells an old camera, and everybody has an opinion about the delivery times. The company, which Marcos Galperin started in 1999 while he was studying in the United States, is now one of the largest businesses in Latin America. But how does it actually work?

At its simplest, Mercado Libre connects people. A seller who has something to sell creates a listing, adds photos and sets a price. A buyer who searches for that product sees the listing, compares it with others and clicks "Buy". The platform takes a commission from the sale, and that commission pays for most of what the company does.

The key, however, is trust. A person who never sees the seller face to face needs a reason to send money to a stranger. So the site uses a reputation system. Each seller has a colour bar that goes from red to green, and every buyer who completes a purchase leaves a rating. Nobody wants a red bar, so the system pushes everyone to behave well.

Payment is the second piece. Mercado Pago, the company's financial arm, holds the buyer's money until the product arrives. If something goes wrong, the platform steps in and usually returns the money. Today Mercado Pago does much more than that. It lets people pay with a QR code in a corner shop, transfer money to a friend or pay the electricity bill. A lot of small businesses that don't have a card terminal accept payments this way.

The third piece is logistics. Mercado Libre runs its own delivery network, Mercado Envíos. A warehouse in the suburbs of Buenos Aires receives products, stores them and sends them out, often on the same day. The company also has its own planes. When a parcel arrives in less than twenty-four hours, it feels almost magical, but it depends on a huge operation that never stops.

Of course, not everything works perfectly. Each user has a complaint: a parcel that takes too long, a seller who doesn't answer, an app that changes its design every few months. Still, the platform does what it promises most of the time, and that explains why almost everyone uses it.

Lesson 3 Agreement with tricky subjects

Levels○ B1● B2 most frequent○ C1

Focuspeople are; most of … + plural; agreement at a distance; no -s on plural subjects

The key idea in one linePeople is plural. Think personas, not gente: people are, people don’t.

The Spanish pullLa gente es↓People are

1Think about

  • Do you think of people as singular or plural? What about la gente?
  • When you say most of my colleagues… or a lot of people…, what verb comes next: is or are, doesn't or don't?
  • Have you ever added an -s to a verb "just in case"?

2You already know

The verb agrees with the head of the subject, not with the nearest noun.

Spanish vs English
SpanishEnglish
La gente es / no confía (singular noun)People are / don't trust (plural noun)
La mayoría de mis clientes estabaMost of my clients were
Mucha gente todavía no recuperóA lot of people haven't recovered yet
The key idea
  • People is the plural of person. Think personas, not gente. If you'd say las personas son, English says people are.
  • Quantifier + of + noun → the noun decides. Most of the students are…, but most of the time is….
  • -s on the verb means ONE. They, humans and people are many, so the verb has no -s.

3Why it goes wrong

  • la gente is singular. When you generalise about society, people doesn't comes straight from la gente no. This is the clearest Spanish transfer in all the data, and it appears at every level.
  • The verb agrees with the nearest noun. In the price of the tickets, the verb should agree with price. In the prices of everything, it should agree with prices, not everything.
  • The quantifier sounds singular. A lot of people starts with a, so the verb comes out singular.
  • doesn't becomes an all-purpose negative. My clients doesn't…, they doesn't….
  • Over-correction. When you're concentrating hard, you sometimes add -s to a plural subject: they works. The rule has become "present verbs need -s" instead of "only after one person or thing".
Typical mistakes
  • "People is very friendly here." → People are
  • "A lot of people doesn't use cash any more." → don't
  • "Most of my clients was happy with the result." → were
  • "They works from home on Fridays." → work

Boca or River? Football and the people who live for it

Ask anyone in Buenos Aires which team they support, and you will rarely hear "I don't really follow football." People here don't just watch the game; they organise their weeks around it. And most people belong to one of two tribes: Boca Juniors or River Plate.

People say that the two clubs represent different worlds. Boca fans are proud of their working-class roots in La Boca, the old port neighbourhood, while River fans are traditionally associated with the wealthier north of the city. Today the reality is more mixed, and there are people on both sides from every social class. But the stereotypes survive, because people love them.

On the day of a superclásico, the atmosphere changes. Most of the city's attention is on one match. A lot of people don't work properly that afternoon, and the streets around the stadium are full hours before kick-off. Fans who travel from other provinces arrive the night before. Since 2013, away fans haven't been allowed into league matches in Argentina, so the home crowd fills the whole stadium.

The rivalry reached a strange peak in 2018, when the two clubs met in the final of the Copa Libertadores. The second leg couldn't be played in Buenos Aires after Boca's team bus was attacked, and the match was moved to Madrid. Most of the supporters who wanted to be there were thousands of kilometres away. River won 3–1, and many Boca fans still refuse to talk about it.

Going to a match isn't cheap. The prices of almost everything around the stadium are high: tickets, food, transport and the shirts that every child wants. The number of fans who can afford a season ticket has fallen, and a lot of families now watch at home or in a bar.

Still, the passion doesn't fade. Children learn the songs before they learn to read. Grandparents who remember the great teams of the past tell the stories again and again. Fans who never miss a game call themselves hinchas, from the verb hinchar, "to swell", and that tells you a lot. People here don't support a team. They inherit it.

Your first milonga in Buenos Aires: questions and answers

What does "milonga" mean?

It means two things. It's a style of music, faster and more playful than tango, and it's also the name of the place where people go to dance. When someone says "I'm going to a milonga tonight," they don't mean a song. They mean a dance hall.

Do I need a partner?

No, you don't. Most people arrive alone or with friends and dance with different partners during the night. Nobody expects you to bring someone.

How does someone invite you to dance?

At a traditional milonga, people don't walk across the room and ask. They use the cabeceo: a look and a small nod of the head from one table to another. If the other person nods back, the invitation is accepted. If they look away, it isn't, and nobody feels embarrassed.

Does the man always lead?

Traditionally, yes, but today it doesn't work that way everywhere. Many milongas welcome women who lead and men who follow. What does matter is that each couple knows who is leading.

Does tango have fixed steps?

Not really. Tango has a basic walk and some common figures, but it doesn't follow a fixed routine. The leader improvises and the follower responds. That's why a beginner doesn't need to memorise long sequences.

What does "tanda" mean?

A tanda is a set of three or four songs of the same style. Between tandas, the DJ plays a short piece of non-tango music, the cortina, and couples leave the floor. You usually dance a whole tanda with the same person.

What happens if I say "thank you" after a song?

Be careful! In many milongas, "gracias" means "I'd like to stop now." If you want to keep dancing, smile and don't say anything.

Where do people dance in Buenos Aires?

Everywhere. There are famous halls in Almagro, Palermo and San Telmo, and on summer evenings people dance outdoors in some squares and parks.

Does it matter what I wear?

It doesn't matter much. Comfortable clothes are fine, but good shoes make a big difference. They need to turn easily on the floor.

Do I need lessons first?

It helps. Many milongas offer a class for beginners an hour before the dancing starts, and it doesn't usually cost much.

2You already know

3Why it goes wrong

  • Describing a temporary change in your routine with the simple present, as in Spanish.
  • Using a state verb in the continuous because the situation feels temporary: I'm needing…
  • Using the continuous with for or since for something that started in the past: I'm not smoking for a long time.
Typical mistakes
  • "This month I work from home because they're painting the office." → I'm working
  • "I'm needing a new phone." → I need
  • "Usually I'm taking the train to work." → I take
  • "I'm not eating meat for two years." → I haven't eaten meat for two years

Lesson 10 Telling a story in the past

Levels● B1 most frequent● B2 most frequent

Focusthe past simple on every verb of a story; had to; coming back to now

The key idea in one lineOnce the story starts in the past, every verb stays there until you come back to now.

The Spanish pullY entonces llego y le digo…↓And then I arrived and told him…

This is the most important lesson in the workbook. In the data, this single mistake was about a quarter of all the verb mistakes at B1 and B2.

1Think about

  • When someone asks How are you? and you answer with a story about yesterday, what tense do you start in?
  • When you tell a long story, do you keep the past until the end? Or does the present come back after a few sentences?
  • How do you say tuve que in English?

2You already know

Finished events in the past take the past simple. Every verb in the story needs it, not just the first one.

Spanish vs English
  • The historic present. Spanish speakers naturally tell stories in the present to make them vivid: "Y entonces llego, y le digo…". English also has a historic present, but only in jokes and very informal stories, and the speaker chooses it for the whole story. Switching back and forth sounds like a mistake.
  • Present relevance. In Spanish, "Tengo plata en ese banco" is acceptable while telling what happened last week. English separates the two: "I had money in that bank" (the story) and "I still have an account there" (now).
  • tuve que = had to. Have to has a past like any other verb. It's had to, never have to, for the past.
The key idea
  • The time expression (yesterday, last week) is not enough. English marks the past on every verb, including verbs after that, what, which, because and when.
  • One mental check: once the story starts in the past, every verb stays in the past until you deliberately come back to now. Use a "now" phrase to mark the return: "…and now I still have an account there."

3Why it goes wrong

  • The question is in the present. How are you? What are you doing? sets a present frame, and the story about yesterday comes out in that frame: "Yesterday I have a meeting…"
  • You say the time once. After yesterday or on Saturday, your brain feels the time is "done" and doesn't mark it on each verb. In the data, more than a quarter of these mistakes came right after a clear past time expression.
  • The event is also a routine. If you did something last Saturday that you also do regularly, the present feels right.
  • The situation still feels ongoing. If the consequences are still with you, the present pulls the verb.
  • Verbs inside other clauses lose the past. The main verb is fine, but the verb after that or what isn't: "He used what I give him."
  • have to is stored as a fixed chunk that never changes.
  • After you stop to find a word, you often restart the sentence in the present.
  • The story gets lively. The more emotional or interesting the story, the more the Spanish habit takes over.
Typical mistakes
  • "Yesterday I have a meeting with a new client." → I had
  • "On Saturday we go to the beach and we eat fish." → we went … we ate
  • "I have to wait an hour at the bank." (yesterday) → I had to wait
  • "He used the file that I send him." → that I sent him

The falls are impressive all year round, but the water level can change a lot. After heavy rain, the volume might be enormous. In a dry period, some falls could be much smaller. You should check the news before you travel.

Probably the noise. At the Devil's Throat you can't hear the person next to you, and you will feel the spray on your face long before you see the water.

Driving Ruta 40: advice before you go

Ruta 40 is Argentina's most famous road. It runs for more than 5,000 kilometres along the Andes, from the far south of Patagonia to the border with Bolivia. Driving even a small part of it is an adventure, and a little planning makes a big difference.

Before you leave

Before you set off, check the state of the road. Some sections are still gravel, and after it rains they can become difficult. Don't leave until you have a spare tyre, plenty of water and a paper map. Once you leave the big towns, your phone won't always have a signal.

Fuel

This is the golden rule: as soon as you see a petrol station, fill up. In some parts of Patagonia the next one may be 300 kilometres away. When the tank is half empty, start looking for fuel. Don't wait until the warning light comes on.

Altitude

In the north, the road climbs above 4,000 metres at the Abra del Acay. When you reach that altitude, you may feel dizzy or get a headache. Drink water and walk slowly when you get out of the car. Many people chew coca leaves until they feel better. After you come down, the symptoms usually disappear.

Wind and weather

In Patagonia the wind is the real challenge. When a truck passes you, hold the wheel firmly. If the wind gets too strong, stop and wait until it calms down. It usually changes by the afternoon.

Accommodation

When you arrive in a small town like Cafayate or Cachi, look for a room before it gets dark. In the high season, book ahead. Once you've found a place, ask the owner about the next stretch of road. Locals always know more than any app.

Animals

Guanacos and rheas often cross the road without warning. As soon as you see one, slow down. They are beautiful, but they don't move out of the way.

Enjoying it

Finally, don't try to drive too much in one day. When you plan your route, allow time for stops. The best moments come when you stop the car, turn off the engine and listen to the silence.

You won't forget this road after you've driven it. Many travellers say they'll come back as soon as they can.

How to survive your first Argentine asado

So your Argentine friends have invited you to a Sunday asado. Congratulations! It's one of the best ways to understand the country. But if you aren't used to eating for five hours, you might need some advice.

Arrive late, but not too late. If the invitation says one o'clock, avoid arriving at one. Nobody will be ready. Most guests plan to arrive about half an hour later. But don't keep making excuses until three, either: the first things off the grill go fast.

Bring something. You don't need to bring meat; the host has probably decided to buy it all himself. But people expect you to bring wine, a dessert or ice. Many hosts don't mind receiving a bottle of fernet, either.

Don't start eating the main course too fast. An asado has stages. It usually starts with chorizo and morcilla in bread, then the offal, and only later the big cuts of beef. Foreigners who are not used to this system often finish eating before the best part arrives. I always recommend taking small portions at the beginning.

Enjoy talking. An asado is more about the conversation than the food. People enjoy arguing about football, politics and family stories, and they keep talking long after the meat has gone. Try to join in, even if your Spanish isn't perfect. Nobody minds hearing a few mistakes.

Learn to clap. At some point, someone will shout "¡Un aplauso para el asador!" and everyone will start clapping for the person who cooked. Don't forget to clap too. The asador has been standing next to the fire for hours, and he wants to hear it.

Be prepared for a long afternoon. People used to spend the whole of Sunday at the table in Argentina, and many families still do. If you need to leave early, mention it at the beginning so that nobody is surprised.

Help at the end. After the meal, offer to help with the dishes. Most hosts will refuse, but they appreciate the gesture.

Finally, don't refuse the second helping. If you want to make a good impression, keep saying yes. You can stop eating when the sobremesa starts. And then, of course, someone will suggest making some mate.

Levels○ B1○ B2

Focusbackshift after a past reporting verb: would, was going to, could

The key idea in one lineWould is the past of will: he said he would call.

The Spanish pullMe dijo que me llama el lunes↓He said he would call me on Monday

1Think about

  • How do you say Me dijo que me llama el lunes in English?
  • How do you say Estaba segura de que iba a terminar a tiempo?
  • Is would only for conditionals?

2You already know

  • After a past reporting verb, will → would, is going to → was going to, can → could.
  • In the same way: I was sure I would…, I thought it was going to…
Spanish vs English
Colloquial Spanish (no backshift)English (backshift)Wrong
Me dijo que me contacta el lunesHe told me he would contact me on Mondayhe contacts me
Estaba segura de que iba a terminarI was sure I would finish / I was going to finishI was sure I will finish

The key idea
  • Would is the past of will, not only "conditional".
  • When the frame is past (told me, was sure, thought, knew, promised), the future inside it moves back: would / was going to.

3Why it goes wrong

  • Colloquial Spanish doesn't backshift. Me dijo que me contacta el lunes sounds completely natural, so you report the words exactly as they were said.
  • would is learned mainly as "conditional", so it doesn't come to mind as the past of will.
  • Telling what someone promised, or what you expected, usually happens inside a longer story, when attention is on the events.
Typical mistakes
  • "He told me he calls me on Monday." → he would call me
  • "I was sure I will pass the exam." → I would pass
  • "They knew what is going to happen." → what was going to happen
  • "She said she can help us." → she could help us

Waiting for the ice bridge to fall

Every few years, the Perito Moreno Glacier advances across a narrow channel of Lago Argentino and touches the land on the other side. It forms a natural dam. The water rises on one side until it digs a tunnel under the ice, and eventually the bridge above the tunnel collapses. It's one of the most spectacular events in Patagonia, and it's almost impossible to predict.

A few years ago, I was in El Calafate when the news came out. A friend sent me a message saying that the tunnel had opened and that the bridge was going to fall any day. I was sure I would see it. I told my boss I would be back at work on Monday, and I took the first bus to the glacier.

When I arrived, a park ranger told us that the bridge would probably fall within a week, but that nobody could say exactly when. She said it might happen in a few hours or in a few days. I asked her if she had ever seen it. She said she had, twice, and that both times it had happened at night, when the footbridges were closed.

I didn't listen. I was convinced that I was going to be the lucky one. I stayed on the footbridges from morning until closing time, staring at the arch of ice. Every time a small piece fell, the crowd screamed, because everyone thought the big moment was going to happen.

At the end of the day, a guide told me that he would call me if anything changed. I said I would come back the next morning. A Brazilian couple next to me said they were going to sleep in their car so that they wouldn't miss it.

That night, at about two in the morning, the bridge collapsed. Only a few people saw it. The next day the news showed videos taken by a park employee. I watched them on my phone at breakfast.

I had promised my friends that I would send them the best photo of my life. Instead, I sent them a link to a news website. My boss asked me if it had been worth the trip. I said it had, but I didn't sound very convincing.

The Teatro Colón: a building that was made for sound

The Teatro Colón in Buenos Aires is considered one of the best opera houses in the world, and its acoustics are often compared with those of the great concert halls of Europe. It was opened in 1908, but the building had been under construction for almost twenty years.

The project was started by the Italian architect Francesco Tamburini, who died in 1891, shortly after the plans were drawn. The work was then continued by his assistant, Vittorio Meano, who was murdered in 1904. Finally, the building was completed by the Belgian architect Jules Dormal. Each architect left his mark, so several styles can be recognised in the final design.

The main hall is shaped like a horseshoe, a design that was typical of Italian opera houses. It is surrounded by seven levels of boxes and galleries. About 2,500 people can be seated, and several hundred more can be accommodated standing. Much of the decoration was brought from Europe: marble, stained glass, mosaics and furniture were shipped across the Atlantic during the construction.

The sound is the building's greatest treasure. Musicians say that even a quiet note can be heard clearly from the highest gallery. The reasons are still discussed by experts, but the shape of the hall, the materials and the upholstered seats are usually mentioned.

Over the decades, the building was damaged by time and by changes that were made without much care. In the early 2000s, a major restoration was planned. The theatre was closed in 2006, and the work was carried out for almost four years. Original colours were uncovered, damaged surfaces were repaired, and the stage machinery was modernised. The theatre was reopened in 2010, in time for the bicentenary of the May Revolution.

Today, the Colón is visited by thousands of tourists every year. Guided tours are offered in several languages, and they must be booked in advance in the high season. Some of the workshops where costumes and sets are made can also be seen on special tours.

New productions are being prepared all the time, and every year the season is followed closely by opera lovers. For many Argentines, the Colón isn't just a theatre. It is seen as a symbol of the city's golden age.

2You already know

  • Mixing tenses in a general truth: If you press this, it stopped.

The unwritten rules of mate

Mate is more than a drink in Argentina. It's a social ritual, and like every ritual, it has rules. Nobody writes them down, but everyone knows them. If you're a visitor, here's what you need to know.

The person who prepares it is the cebador. If you're in a group, one person prepares the mate and serves everyone. The cebador fills the gourd with hot water, drinks the first one or two, and then passes it around the circle. If the first mate tastes bitter, that's normal. That's why the cebador drinks it.

You drink it all. If someone hands you a mate, you drink all the water, until the straw makes a noise. Then you give it back to the cebador. If you keep it for too long, someone will say "¡No es un micrófono!" ("It isn't a microphone!"). You're expected to drink, not to give a speech.

Don't move the straw. If you move the bombilla, the yerba gets blocked, and the cebador won't be happy. People will forgive a foreigner once, but if you do it twice, they'll start teaching you.

"Thank you" means "no more". This is the most famous rule. If you say "gracias" when you hand back the mate, it means you don't want another one. If you want to keep drinking, just give it back with a smile. Many visitors say "thank you" politely, and then they wonder why nobody offers them another.

Water temperature matters. If the water boils, it burns the yerba and the taste becomes bitter. If you're preparing mate at home, take the kettle off before it boils.

Everyone shares the same straw. If that makes you uncomfortable, you can politely say no. Nobody will be offended. But if you accept, you'll probably feel like part of the group.

Sweet or bitter? In the north and in some families, people add sugar. If you ask for sugar in some groups, they'll tease you a little. But if you prefer it sweet, ask anyway.

If you spend a weekend with Argentine friends, someone will prepare mate within the first hour. If you follow these rules, you'll be invited again. And if you break one, don't worry. That's how everyone learns.

  • This is one of the few areas where C1 learners make more mistakes than B2. Higher-level speakers attempt more hypothetical reasoning, so they meet more chances to go wrong.

What if…? Three Argentine stories that could have been different

History is full of moments when one small change would have changed everything. Argentines love to play this game, especially with football. Here are three "what ifs".

What if Maradona hadn't used his hand?

In the 1986 World Cup quarter-final against England, Diego Maradona scored two goals in four minutes. The first was the famous "Hand of God". The second, a run past half the English team, was later voted the goal of the century. If the referee had seen the handball, the first goal wouldn't have counted. Would Argentina still have won the match? Probably. But if the "Hand of God" hadn't existed, Maradona's story would be less complicated, and English fans wouldn't still talk about it with so much anger.

What if Messi had stayed in Rosario?

When Messi was a child, his family couldn't afford his growth hormone treatment. If Barcelona hadn't offered to pay for it, he would have stayed in Argentina. Maybe a local club would have found the money. Maybe not. If he had stayed, he might not be the player we know today. And if he weren't the player we know, Argentina might not have won the World Cup in 2022. That's a thought most Argentines prefer not to have.

What if I lived in Patagonia?

The third "what if" is personal. People often ask me where I would live if I could choose any place in the country. My answer never changes: El Chaltén, at the foot of Mount Fitz Roy. If I lived there, I would walk in the mountains every morning. I'd have a small wooden house with a big window facing the peaks, and I'd run a little café for hikers. I wouldn't need a car. I'd grow vegetables in a greenhouse, because nothing grows outside in that wind.

Would I be happy? I think so. I don't know if I would call it a perfect life, though. If I lived there, I would miss my friends, the theatres on Corrientes and the noise of Buenos Aires. If the winters weren't so long and dark, I'd move tomorrow.

Maybe that's the point of "what if" questions. If we didn't imagine other lives, we wouldn't value the one we have. And if Maradona hadn't scored that goal, we would have one story less to argue about.

A final word

If you've reached this point, you've worked through 30 lessons on structures you already knew. I hope that now you also know when they break, and why. That's the part no grammar book teaches.

The work doesn't end here. Mistakes like these don't disappear because you understand them. They disappear slowly, as you notice them again and again in your own speech. So if you have the chance, go back to the lessons that were hardest for you, write the texts again, and if you can, record yourself telling a story or explaining something you know well.
