Unit 1 · Telling the past

Lesson 1 Telling a Finished Story

Focus

past simple: regular -ed and irregular verbs; didn’t / did; first, then, after that, in the end

Watch for

“In São Paulo, I get up very early.” · “We have a lunch near the beach.” · “So we return and we stay in the hotel.”

The Spanish pull

Me levanto, tomo el tren…

↓

I got up, I took the train…

The key idea in one line

A finished story stays in the past, from the first verb to the last.

1Think aboutbefore you start

Discuss or think about these questions.

  • Have you ever been to a big sports event? What was it like?
  • What is the best day of your last holiday? What did you do?
  • In Spanish, people sometimes tell a story in the present: "me levanto, tomo el tren…". Do you do this?

2Learnthe past simple for finished stories

When a story is finished, every verb about it goes in the past simple. In Spanish you can tell a story in the present; in English you can't.

✗ Present (wrong for a finished story)✓ Past simple
Yesterday I get up at six.Yesterday I got up at six.
We have lunch at a bar.We had lunch at a bar.
We return at ten.We returned at ten.

Regular verbs: add -ed.

walk → walked · stay → stayed · arrive → arrived · stop → stopped · start → started · cry → cried

Common irregular verbs from the text:

VerbPastVerbPast
get upgot upfindfound
havehadcomecame
taketookfeelfelt
gowentloselost
buyboughtsingsang
bewas / wereputput

Negatives and questions use didn't / did + the base verb:

He didn't win. (not didn't won) · Did you buy a cap? (not Did you bought)

Sequencing words help the reader follow the story:

First, … Then … After that, … In the end, …

Race Day at Interlagos

Sunday, 9 November

What a day! I'm writing this in bed at the hotel because I can't sleep. I'm too excited.

I got up at six because I didn't want to miss anything. First, I had a quick breakfast at the hotel: coffee, bread with cheese and a big glass of orange juice. My brother came down at half past six, still half asleep, with his Argentina flag on his shoulders. My husband and my nephew stayed in bed. They wanted to sleep a little more and meet us later at the circuit.

Then my brother and I took the train to Interlagos. The train was full of fans with flags and T-shirts from every country. A group of Brazilian boys sang songs all the way, and some Mexican fans answered with their own songs. Everyone laughed. It felt like a football match.

We arrived at the circuit at nine. It was already hot. We found our seats, bought two bottles of water and put on sunscreen. After that, we walked around and looked at the shops. I bought a cap and a key ring for my daughter. At twelve, my husband and my nephew finally arrived, with sandwiches for everyone.

The race started at two in the afternoon. When the cars passed in front of us for the first time, the noise was incredible. I felt it in my chest! My nephew covered his ears and smiled at the same time.

The race was very exciting. In the middle, it suddenly rained for ten minutes, and two cars crashed in the first corner. Nobody got hurt, but the safety car came out and everything changed. One driver lost five positions in one lap. Another driver, who started at the back, finished on the podium. Nobody expected that. My favourite driver didn't win, but he finished in the points, and we screamed like children.

After the race, we stayed in our seats for an hour and watched the celebrations on the big screen. Then we walked back to the station with thousands of people. It took us two hours to get back to the hotel. We stopped at a little restaurant on the way and had pizza and cold drinks.

In the end, I was tired, sunburnt and very, very happy. This was my second time at Interlagos, and it was even better than the first. I want to go back next year.

Lesson 2 When It Happened

Focus

Watch for

The Spanish pull

A la noche

↓

The key idea in one line

A Saturday in the Sierras

Last Saturday, my husband and I visited Villa General Belgrano for the second time. It's a small town in the hills of Córdoba, about an hour and a half from the city. It looks like a village in Germany, with wooden houses, red roofs, flowers on the windows and beer gardens. German families started the town about ninety years ago, and you can still see their culture everywhere.

We left Córdoba at about eight in the morning. The road was quiet, and the hills looked beautiful in the morning light. On the way, we stopped at a little café by the road and had breakfast: coffee with milk and medialunas, still warm. We didn't have lunch until much later, so it was a good idea.

We arrived at ten. In the morning, we walked around the town centre and looked at the shops. There are shops with chocolate, cheese, smoked meat and handmade wooden toys. I bought a box of alfajores for my mother and a jar of jam for my sister. My husband bought a beer glass with the name of the town on it. He collects them.

At one o'clock, we had lunch at a German restaurant on the main street. The waiter wore traditional clothes. My husband ordered sausages with potatoes and red cabbage, and I had goulash with spätzle, a kind of small pasta. It was delicious, but too much food! For dessert, we shared a piece of apple strudel.

In the afternoon, we drove to a river ten minutes from the town and sat in the sun for an hour. We did nothing. We just listened to the water and the birds. It was perfect.

In October, the town has a big beer festival, the National Beer Festival. Thousands of people come from all over Argentina, and there is music, dancing and a parade. We went once, in 2019, and it was really fun, but very crowded. We waited forty minutes for one sausage! This time it was quiet, and I liked it more.

At five, we had a coffee and a piece of cake in a tea house. Then we started the drive back. We stopped at a viewpoint at sunset and took some photos. We got home at about nine at night, very relaxed.

On Sunday, we slept until noon. Next month, we want to visit La Cumbrecita, another small town near there.

Lost in Buenos Aires

Day 2: The day I got lost (and found the best pizza in the city)

I arrived in Buenos Aires on Thursday. It's a huge city, with more than thirteen million people in the metropolitan area, and it's very different from my small town in Canada. On my first day, I stayed in Palermo, near my hotel. I walked in the parks, sat at a café and went to bed early.

On my second day, I wanted to go to San Telmo. It's the oldest neighbourhood in the city, with old houses, antique shops and a famous street market on Sundays. People dance tango in the square in front of the market. I really wanted to see that.

The receptionist at my hotel explained the route to me. "Take the subway to the city centre, then change to another line," she said. It sounded easy.

Big mistake: I got on the wrong line! I didn't notice for six stations. When I finally looked at the map on the wall, I didn't recognise any of the names. I got off at a station I didn't know and walked up the stairs to the street. I was in the middle of a busy avenue, with buses, taxis and hundreds of people. And of course, there was no internet on my phone.

I went into a small newsagent's on the corner and asked for help in my terrible Spanish. The man was very kind. He drew a map on a piece of paper. "Walk to the corner, turn left, and the bus stop is in front of the bank," he said. "Take the 29. Get off at Plaza Dorrego." I thanked him about ten times.

I waited at the bus stop for twenty minutes, but the bus didn't come. Next to the stop, there was a small pizzeria, and the smell was amazing. I was hungry, so I went in. I sat at a table by the window. On the wall, there were old photos of football players and tango singers. The waiter recommended the pizza with mozzarella and onions. It was the best pizza of my life: thick, with lots of cheese. I also tried fainá, a kind of thin bread made with chickpeas. Argentines put it on top of the pizza!

In the end, I got on the bus at four and got to San Telmo at five. The market was closing, and the tango dancers were going home. But I didn't care. I was full and happy, and I had a great story to tell.

Tomorrow I'm going back to San Telmo. This time, I'm taking a taxi.

The Asado

In Argentina, Sunday is the day for an asado. An asado is a barbecue, but it's also much more than food: it's a family meeting that lasts all afternoon. Argentines say that you can't understand the country without understanding the asado.

In my family, the asado is always at my parents' house. They have a big garden with an old brick grill. My father is the asador, the person who cooks the meat. It's an important job, and in many families it passes from father to son, or from mother to daughter. My father learned from his father, and now my brother is learning from him.

My father starts the fire at eleven. He uses wood, not charcoal, because he says the taste is better. Nobody can touch the grill. It's his job, and he takes it very seriously. He stands next to the fire for three hours with a glass of red wine in his hand.

The rest of the family usually arrives at twelve. My sister's kids play in the garden with the dog, and my mother's kitchen is full of people making salads and drinking mate. Argentines eat a lot of meat, but the salads are important too: lettuce and tomato, potato and egg salad, and rice salad. There's always bread on the table, and a bowl of chimichurri, a green sauce made with parsley, garlic, oil and vinegar.

We eat at about two. The asado comes in parts. First come the chorizos and the morcillas, a kind of black sausage. People eat them in bread, as a choripán. Then come the ribs, the flank steak and the other cuts. Every family has a favourite. In my family, it's the vacío.

When the meat is on the table, everyone gives the asador a round of applause. That's a tradition! My father always says "It's nothing," but he loves it.

After lunch, we have coffee and dessert. My aunt's flan with dulce de leche is the best in the world. Then come the long conversations about football, politics and the family. Sometimes the adults play cards, and the kids fall asleep on the sofa.

My friends from other countries are always surprised. They think two meals in one day are enough. In Argentina, the asado is only one meal, but it takes six hours.

If you visit Córdoba, you need to know one word: cuarteto. It's the most popular music in the city, and people love it so much. You hear it in taxis, in shops, at weddings, at football matches and at every party.

Cuarteto started in Córdoba in the 1940s. At first, there were only four musicians in each band, with a piano, an accordion, a violin and a double bass. That's why it's called "cuarteto". The first famous band was Cuarteto Leo, and many people say it started everything. The music has a fast, happy rhythm. People say it sounds like "tunga-tunga, tunga-tunga".

In the beginning, rich people didn't like cuarteto. They said it was music for poor people. Radio stations didn't play it, and there weren't many places for the bands. But workers and young people loved it, and they danced to it every weekend in clubs in the neighbourhoods. Slowly, cuarteto became the music of the whole city.

There are two very famous names in cuarteto. The first is La Mona Jiménez, a singer with long curly hair and crazy colourful clothes. The second is Rodrigo, a young singer with a beautiful voice. In the 1990s, he became famous all over Argentina, not only in Córdoba. His songs are still really popular today. You don't need to be a good dancer, and you don't need to know the songs. You just need comfortable shoes and lots of energy. And if someone asks you to dance, say yes!

Cyber Monday Report

Summary

This year, Cyber Monday was our best sales event ever. Total sales went up by 35% compared with last year, and we had more customers than in any other week in the company's history. Our online store had more visits than on Black Friday, and our Mercado Libre account was the busiest it has ever been. Monday night, between nine and midnight, was the busiest time of the event.

Best and worst products

Whey protein had the best performance again. It sold better than all our other products, especially the chocolate flavour. The vanilla flavour was less popular, but it still sold well. Vitamins were our second-best category. Sales of vitamin D and magnesium went up by 50%, maybe because more people are thinking about their health.

Creatine was a nice surprise. Last year it was one of our weakest products, but this year it was the third most popular. Pre-workout drinks had the worst performance: sales went down by 10%. They are more expensive than the competition's, and many customers looked for cheaper options. Protein bars were also disappointing.

Prices and the competition

Our prices were lower than last year's, but they weren't the lowest on Mercado Libre. Two competitors had bigger discounts on protein bars and pre-workout drinks. One of them also offered free shipping on all orders, and that was more attractive for many customers. Next time, we need better offers on these products, or we need to give something extra, like a free shaker.

Customer service

We had more questions than usual, about 40% more than on a normal week. But our answers were faster: the average time was twelve minutes, better than last year's twenty-five minutes. Returns were lower than last year: only 2% of orders. Most returns were because of the wrong flavour or the wrong size. Our customer ratings were also higher than before.

Next steps

Our most important goal for Christmas is to reduce the price of pre-workout drinks and to send orders faster. Deliveries to the south of the country were the slowest, so we need to talk to the delivery company. We also want to make a bigger, cheaper pack of protein bars.

Black Friday was good, but Cyber Monday was even better. Now we want the best December in the company's history!

Next week, I'm going to the hills with the family. I can't wait.

I miss you. Write soon!
